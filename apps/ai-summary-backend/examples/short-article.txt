The Quiet Comeback of the Bicycle

Cities around the world are rediscovering the bicycle. Over the past decade, urban cycling has grown steadily, driven by rising fuel prices, crowded public transport and a renewed interest in personal health. Paris, Bogotá and Copenhagen have each added hundreds of kilometres of protected bike lanes, and in several of these cities cycling now accounts for more daily trips than private cars in the city centre.

The shift is not only about infrastructure. Electric bicycles have widened the group of people willing to cycle. Hills, long distances and hot weather are less of an obstacle when a motor helps with the hardest part of the ride. Older riders and parents carrying children are among the fastest-growing groups of e-bike owners.

Businesses have noticed. Cargo bikes now deliver parcels, groceries and restaurant meals in dense neighbourhoods, where they are often faster than vans because they avoid traffic and do not need parking. Some logistics companies report that a single cargo bike can replace one delivery van for routes under ten kilometres.

There are still challenges. Bike theft remains common, winter weather discourages riders in northern cities, and conflicts between cyclists, pedestrians and drivers continue where lanes are poorly designed. Experts agree that the most successful cities treat cycling as part of a complete transport network, connecting bike lanes to train stations, offering secure parking and lowering speed limits for cars.

If current trends continue, researchers expect cycling to double its share of urban trips in many European cities by 2035, cutting emissions, reducing noise and freeing up public space that is currently used for parking.
